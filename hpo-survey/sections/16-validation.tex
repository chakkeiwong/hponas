\chapter{The validation suite}
\label{sec:validation}

A tuning product can fool its builders the same way a configuration
fools a tuner: by being evaluated on the problems it was tuned on.
This document has met the trap twice, as seed overfitting in
Chapter~\ref{sec:workloads} and as the proxy bias of
Figure~\ref{fig:curves}, and the defense is the same both times:
decide the evaluation before the thing being evaluated exists. This
chapter is that decision. It collects every falsification promise
made in Chapters~\ref{sec:modelfree} through~\ref{sec:product} into
numbered tests with pass rules, prices the expensive ones, and ties
each build tier's completion to named entries rather than to the
feeling of being done.

\section{Three layers}

The suite runs at three costs, and a test's layer is part of its
identity.

Layer one is cheap and broad: surrogate and tabular benchmarks
(YAHPO-Gym, HPOBench, and the RL-specific ARLBench), where a full
tuner comparison costs CPU-minutes because the training runs were
paid for once by others \citep{pfisterer2022yahpo,
eggensperger2021hpobench, becktepe2024arlbench}, plus recorded-curve
replays in the style of Syne Tune's simulator
(Chapter~\ref{sec:libraries}), where a scheduler's decisions are
re-executed against stored learning curves in seconds. A scheduler
bug found in a CPU-minute replay is a bug not found in a GPU-week
study; layer one exists to make that sentence routine. The
multi-fidelity-with-priors suite from the PriorBand line covers the
prior-handling code specifically \citep{mallik2023priorband}.

Layer two is realistic and ours: one pinned task per workload
family. A PPO task on a Brax environment; a flow-plus-HMC sampler
task scored by ESS per gradient under divergence and
split-$\widehat{R}$ vetoes, with a reference posterior computed once
at great length; a Hamiltonian network task scored on prediction
error and energy drift. Layer two is where verdicts about \emph{our}
workloads are settled, at GPU prices, under the statistical
discipline of Section~\ref{sec:val-protocol}.

Layer three is standing: diagnostics that run inside every real
study the product ever executes. The rank correlation between rung
scores and final scores is logged always
(Chapter~\ref{sec:multifidelity}); the seed protocol's presence or
explicitly recorded absence is part of every study row
(Chapter~\ref{sec:contracts}). Layer three is how the suite keeps
learning after release, and it costs nothing because the store
records it anyway.

\section{The statistical protocol}
\label{sec:val-protocol}

Every layer-two comparison follows one protocol, copied from the
discipline of \citet{eimer2023hyperparameters} and from this
document's own warnings. Fixed tuning seeds and disjoint test seeds.
Budgets matched on measured cost, with the tuner's own overhead
included in the bill. Results reported as performance-over-budget
curves with uncertainty bands, never as single numbers. And a
repetition count that is derived rather than declared: a pilot
campaign estimates the between-run variance, and the sample size
follows from that variance and the entry's decision margin.

Derived, though, is only as good as the derivation, and an earlier draft
of this paragraph stopped at the gesture. It named a pilot, a variance,
a margin, and 80 percent power, and left out everything an implementer
would need to reproduce the number: whether the test is paired or
unpaired, one- or two-sided, on means or on a curve summary; which
variance estimator; how the arms are allocated; how small a pilot is too
small; and what to do when the answer exceeds the budget. Two
statisticians following that text would size the same campaign
differently, which means the sample size was not actually preregistered.

So each entry fixes the whole calculation in
\texttt{validation/protocols.json} before its pilot runs. The estimand,
the direction, and the sidedness come from the entry. The sample size is
obtained by Monte Carlo simulation under the entry's own hierarchy—task
cluster, then paired study block—rather than from a closed-form
two-sample formula, because the estimand is a cluster-level paired
difference and the closed form does not describe it. The pilot is at
least five replicates per arm per side, raised from the three an earlier
draft implied, since a three-replicate variance estimate is too noisy to
size anything on. And the sizing runs against the upper confidence bound
on the pilot variance rather than its point estimate, so that pilot noise
inflates the sample instead of shrinking it. Pilot observations do not
enter the confirmatory estimate; the two campaigns use disjoint seed
sets. The power target is 80 percent by default and 90 percent for V04's
tier-1 component and for V08, whose builds carry enough sunk cost that a
false demotion is the more expensive error.

The ten repetitions this document has quoted since
Chapter~\ref{sec:workloads} are a planning heuristic for the
calendar and the budget, not a substitute for that calculation.
Where the pilot shows low variance against a wide margin, fewer
repetitions suffice and the protocol records why; where it shows
high variance against a narrow one, the budget rises or the claim
softens, and the entry says which.

Because the budget is finite, every entry also carries a maximum
sample size, and reaching it without crossing the decision threshold
is a third outcome alongside pass and fail: \emph{inconclusive at
budgeted precision}. That is a real verdict with a real consequence,
which is that the capability does not become a default on evidence
that never arrived. The verdict also needs an end, which an earlier
draft did not give it: a state that neither passes nor fails and has no
funder and no deadline is a capability parked indefinitely next to a
release decision. An inconclusive result therefore permits at most one
replication, whose budget, owner, and stopping rule are fixed before the
first confirmatory result is seen. After that replication the verdict is
terminal and resolves to the consequence the entry's register names. Pilot variance estimates are recorded before the
full campaign begins and are not revised during it, since a variance
estimate updated mid-campaign is a decision threshold updated
mid-campaign wearing a disguise. V08 remains the one entry whose arms
are too expensive for any of this at full strength; it runs three
repetitions per arm per side under the restricted question,
asymmetric demotion rule, and release semantics of
Section~\ref{sec:val-v08}, and any future exception is written into
this section before its campaign runs, never after its results are
seen.

Verdicts then come in three strengths, and the suite's reports use
the distinction explicitly. Hard vetoes (a diverged run promoted, a
veto-failing configuration surviving, a protected seed leaking into
tuning) fail a test regardless of scores. Descriptive differences
(one curve above another within overlapping bands) are recorded and
never rank anything. A ranking is claimed only when the repetitions
support it statistically; when a result shows one method ahead
within noise, the honest summary is that they tied, and a tie
against a simpler method is a loss for the complex one wherever the
register says so (V04, V09). The suite exists to catch regressions
and to rank where gaps are real, not to manufacture leaderboard
drama.

\subsection{Register shorthand resolved}

The table above uses unquantified phrases to fit on one page; this
section pins each one to a concrete rule before any entry runs.

\textbf{``Within tolerance'' (V01):} A fixed percentage band is the
wrong instrument here, and an earlier draft of this chapter used one: it
asked that anytime and final scores differ by no more than $0.5\%$ of
the reference method's final value. The references are stochastic, so
that band tests a determinism they do not have—two correct
implementations of the same searcher differ by more than $0.5\%$ across
seeds routinely, and a band tight enough to catch a wiring bug flags
correct code as often as broken code. V01 instead compares the two
implementations in distribution: final and anytime score vectors at
shared seeds are compared by a two-sample distributional test, and the
entry passes when the wrapper's score distribution is indistinguishable
from the reference's. The comparison is loose on any single run and
tight on the ensemble, which is the shape the question actually
has—V01 gates implementation correctness, not optimizer superiority.

\textbf{``Within noise'' (V06, V11):} The claim these entries make is
one-sided—that early stopping does not \emph{lose} quality, that a
prior does not \emph{harm} a study that gets bad advice—so the test is
one-sided non-inferiority, not the two-sided containment an earlier
draft specified. That draft asked for the 95\% interval of the
difference to lie entirely inside $[-\delta, +\delta]$, which is an
equivalence criterion: it rejects an arm for being materially
\emph{better} as well as materially worse. Requiring ASHA not to
outperform full-fidelity search is not a property anyone wants. Each
entry now declares its direction, and the criterion is that the lower
bound of the one-sided 95\% interval on the direction-normalized
difference clears $-\delta$.

The margins are product tolerances rather than statistical quantities,
and they cannot be read off observed noise: $\delta$ is the largest
degradation that would not change what the product recommends. For V06
it is 5 percent; for V11's wrong-prior arm, 10 percent. Both are set
before the campaigns for that reason. The scale is a range
normalization against bounds declared in the task registry, not a
percentage of the baseline's value, because reinforcement-learning
return is routinely zero or negative and a percentage of it is either
undefined or unstable; a denominator estimated during the campaign is a
decision threshold that moves with the data.

The distinction from an interval that merely overlaps zero is the whole
point. Overlapping zero says a difference was not detected, which a
small enough sample says about everything; a bound clear of $-\delta$
says any difference that survives is too small to care about. A result
that fails to clear $-\delta$ but does not establish a loss either is
recorded as inconclusive, not as a tie. V11 additionally splits in two,
because its two claims have different shapes: the good-prior arm makes
a superiority claim against no prior, and only the wrong-prior arm is a
non-inferiority question. One margin cannot serve both.

\textbf{``A fraction of compute'' (V06):} Early stopping reaches the
same final quality, within the non-inferiority margin above, at no
more than one-third the total compute of full-fidelity search.
The savings claim is about resources consumed, not about an
idealization of them, but an earlier draft of this section made the
opposite error and put queue wait, wall-clock, and accelerator-seconds
into one bill before declaring that the threshold applies to
accelerator-seconds. Those are different quantities. Queue wait
consumes no accelerator time at all, and folding it into a count of
accelerator-seconds requires a billing conversion the draft never
stated. Worse, the resulting rule could pass a configuration whose
active device time is low and whose queue and idle time is
unacceptable, which is the opposite of the user-facing claim.

The quantities are therefore reported in separate columns and one of
them is named primary. The primary is billed resource-seconds: the
device time the user is actually charged for, which includes
allocated-but-idle time, startup, checkpoint save and load, retries,
and preempted work. Active accelerator-seconds, CPU-seconds, elapsed
wall-clock, and queue wait are each reported alongside it as
secondaries and are never mixed into the primary. Billed
resource-seconds is primary because the claim is about what a user
pays: active accelerator-seconds understates a wasteful schedule, and
wall-clock charges an arm for unrelated cluster contention. Shared
startup and queue time is attributed per trial by allocation record
rather than divided evenly, both arms run under the same scheduling and
accounting policy, and a comparison across hardware classes is void.

Full-run equivalents survive as a normalized secondary for readability,
computed by dividing total cost by the mean cost of one full-fidelity
trial in the baseline arm, and they are never the number the gate
reads—that denominator is itself estimated, and a point estimate of a
one-third ratio is not a gate. The cost ratio carries a paired cluster
bootstrap interval and the gate reads its upper bound. V06 passes only
when both conditions hold: quality is non-inferior within its margin
\emph{and} the upper bound on the cost ratio clears one third. Passing
on quality while cost overruns is not a pass, and neither is the
reverse.

\textbf{``Measured trial savings'' (V12):} The warm-started study
reaches the same final objective value in at least 20\% fewer trials
than a cold start on the same task. Both arms use the same searcher;
only the initialization differs. Below 20\%, warm starting is not
worth defaulting on.

\textbf{``Hypervolume with uncertainty'' (V09):} Report hypervolume
over budget with 95\% confidence bands from paired cluster bootstrap
resampling. The decision is made on the paired difference between
qLogNEHVI and each comparator, not on whether their marginal intervals
touch. An earlier draft of this section demoted the premium method when
its interval overlapped the simpler method's, which is not a test of a
difference at all: two marginal intervals can overlap while the paired
difference is decisively nonzero, because pairing removes the
between-instance variance that makes each marginal wide, and they can
separate while the paired difference is not significant. Reading overlap
as a verdict throws away the pairing the design paid for and answers a
question nobody asked.

The reference point and the objective normalization are fixed from
declared domain bounds in the task registry before any campaign data
exists. Hypervolume is not scale-free—it moves with the reference point
and with how the objectives are scaled—so a reference point chosen after
seeing results is a threshold chosen after seeing results. qLogNEHVI
must clear both the Chebyshev scalarization and the wrapped NSGA-II at
the gate's adjusted level to hold its tier slot; a tie against the
simpler method demotes it to an option, as the register says.

\textbf{``Enough studies'' (V12):} The run store must contain at
least ten completed studies from the same model family (same
architecture class, same task domain) before V12 runs. Below that,
the warm-start query has no data to learn from and the test is
meaningless.

All numerical gates (one-third for V06, 20\% for V12, the false-cull
and recall bounds for V10, 1.01 for V13) are judgments recorded before
the data arrives, not fit to results. The V10 and V13 protocols later in
this chapter set their thresholds explicitly; the phrases above inherit
the same discipline. Every one of them, together with each entry's
estimand, direction, family, power target, maximum sample, missingness
rule, and release consequence, is recorded in
\texttt{validation/protocols.json}, which is the machine-readable
authority this prose is checked against. That file exists because this
chapter drifted from a set of corrections it had already accepted, in
five separate entries, and nothing detected the drift until review; a
rule that lives only in prose is a rule with no way to notice when it
changes.

\subsection{Sampling levels and paired designs}

A tuning comparison has more than one source of randomness, and
conflating them is the fastest way to a confident wrong answer. Five
levels are in play: the optimizer seed (which tuning run), the
workload training seed (which initialization or environment
instance), the evaluation seed (which test episodes), the task
(which benchmark), and the configuration draw (which candidates the
searcher happened to try). Treating the leaves as independent samples
when the branches are shared is pseudoreplication: it shrinks the
reported interval without adding information, and every gate
downstream inherits the false precision.

The first defense is pairing. Where a comparison admits it, the
methods share the optimizer seed set, the task and environment
instances, and any baseline configurations drawn once and evaluated
by all arms. Pairing removes the between-instance variance from the
contrast rather than averaging over it, which buys power at no extra
compute, and it applies wherever the methods being compared consume
seeds the same way. Where they do not, because a population method
spends seeds on a schedule a sequential method has no analogue for,
the entry says so and falls back to unpaired sampling with the larger
sample size the power calculation then demands. Which case an entry is
in is declared before its data exists. ``Fall back to unpaired if the
seed-level correlation breaks'' is not a rule unless \emph{breaks} was
defined in advance, and an entry that chooses its pairing after seeing
which choice looks better has chosen its threshold after seeing the
data.

Uncertainty is computed by hierarchical bootstrap rather than by
resampling individual trials, and the unit matters more than the
nesting. The resampling unit is the complete study replicate: tasks are
resampled with replacement, and within each sampled task, complete
paired blocks are resampled with replacement, with the arms inside a
block held together. An earlier draft said only that runs were
resampled within sampled tasks, which reads as resampling each arm
independently—and that destroys exactly the covariance pairing was
introduced to exploit, quietly widening every paired interval in the
chapter. Individual trials are never the unit: a searcher chooses its
later trials in light of its earlier ones, so trials inside a run are
adaptively selected and are not exchangeable.

The statistic of interest is recomputed on each of 10,000 bootstrap
samples and reported at empirical percentiles. Ten thousand draws bound
the Monte Carlo error of the interval, not the number of clusters
underneath it; with roughly ten clusters the interval is wide however
many draws are taken, and no draw count converts a pinned-task result
into a workload-family one. An entry whose acceptance set holds a single
task says so and limits its claim to that task, which is why V08 and
V09 report pinned-task results rather than family-level ones. Failed and
stopped runs need the same discipline: each entry declares whether an
incomplete replicate is dropped with its paired block, carried forward
at its last observed value, or scored as worst case, and a campaign
losing more than a tenth of its planned replicates in any arm is void
rather than merely noisy.

\subsection{Development and acceptance tasks}

The trap this chapter opened with applies to the suite itself. A
pinned task that implementers run daily while debugging stops being a
held-out measurement of anything; it becomes the thing the
implementation was tuned on, and the gate it anchors measures fit to
the validation set. So each workload family carries two disjoint task
sets. Development tasks are named in week one of tier~0, recorded in
the validation manifest, and free to run as often as anyone likes.
Acceptance tasks are chosen by an engineer not implementing the
capability under test, revealed at gate time, and recorded in the
gate manifest with their data checksums.

Both numbers are reported, and the gap between them is itself a
diagnostic: a capability strong on development tasks and weak on
acceptance tasks has overfit the suite, which is a finding rather
than a mystery. Promotion decisions weigh the acceptance results,
because those are the only ones the implementation has not seen.

Task sets rotate on a version, not on a feeling. When a tier's gates
are recorded, new tasks enter development and some older development
tasks may migrate to acceptance for the next tier, with version
numbers and dates in the task registry. The registry is what makes
task-selection bias auditable after the fact, which matters
precisely because the person selecting is usually the person hoping.

Two details decide whether that audit can actually be performed.
``Chosen by an engineer not implementing the capability'' is a role
separation, and role separation is not a sampling design: it leaves the
task universe, the eligibility filters, the stratification, and the
tie-breaking unstated, so a selection made in good faith is still
unreproducible. The registry therefore records the selection
\emph{algorithm} and the candidate set, committed by hash before the
implementation freeze, with the selected identifiers revealed at gate
time as before. Blinding protects against reading results early;
committing the algorithm protects against the discretion that survives
blinding.

The other detail is exposure. Migrating a development task into
acceptance carries everything the implementers learned from it while
debugging against it, so a task cannot be laundered by relabeling.
Every task carries an exposure history in the registry, naming which
capabilities have seen it and in which role, and a task that has been
developed against does not become acceptance evidence for the same
capability. Nor may a prior tier's acceptance result be recycled as new
evidence at a later gate. And one task per family cannot carry a
family-level claim however carefully it was chosen: either the acceptance
set holds several independent tasks, or the claim names the pinned task
and stops there.

\subsection{Multiple comparisons and endpoints}

Several entries ask more than one question. V04 compares searchers
across tasks and budget points; V09 crosses objectives with
comparators; V13 inspects many posterior marginals at once. Twenty
independent comparisons at the conventional threshold produce one
spurious winner on average, which is enough to promote a searcher
that does nothing.

Each entry therefore predeclares one primary endpoint, the single
comparison the tier's completion depends on, and labels the rest. But
one primary endpoint per entry is not one comparison per decision, and
an earlier draft of this chapter drew exactly that false inference: it
judged each primary at $\alpha = 0.05$ uncorrected on the grounds that
there was only one of it. There is only one per entry, and a gate
decides several entries at once. Six independent primaries at an
uncorrected threshold carry a family-wise false-positive probability of
$1 - 0.95^6 \approx 0.26$, and the full suite of fifteen carries
$1 - 0.95^{15} \approx 0.54$. A gate that promotes on those terms is
promoting noise about half the time it promotes.

The family is therefore the gate, not the entry. Every decision-bearing
primary at a gate joins that gate's confirmatory family and is
controlled family-wise at $\alpha = 0.05$ by the Holm--Bonferroni
stepdown procedure \citep{holm1979simple}, which is uniformly more
powerful than plain Bonferroni at the same guarantee and needs no
assumption about dependence among the tests. Secondary comparisons that
feed a ranking claim are controlled in the same family. The gate, and
not the program, is the unit because gates are separate decisions taken
at different times on different data with different release
consequences; a single family stretched across thirty weeks would cost
the tier-0 floor tests real power for no inferential gain.

Two kinds of entry sit outside the families and consume no
$\alpha$. Hard vetoes decide on a deterministic criterion rather than a
rejected null: a divergent run promoted, a veto-failing configuration
surviving, a protected seed reaching the searcher, a mutation score
below its floor. Reproduction checks likewise either reproduce or do
not. Neither is a ranking claim, and correcting them would trade real
power for a guarantee that was never at risk. Comparisons that rank
nothing and decide nothing, the descriptive kind this chapter has
already licensed, need no correction either, and saying so is what
keeps multiplicity control from becoming a tax on honest reporting.

Where an entry must beat more than one comparator to earn its slot, the
entry declares an intersection--union rule and must clear every
comparator at the adjusted level; V09 against both Chebyshev
scalarization and NSGA-II is the worked case. Where beating any one
comparator suffices, the comparators enter the family as separate
members instead. And a budget point may not be chosen after inspecting
a performance-over-budget curve, which is a repeated look wearing the
costume of a summary: an entry whose decision rests on a curve names a
summary functional, such as normalized area under the regret curve,
before its campaign begins.

V04's tier-0 primary endpoint, as the worked instance: whether the
tier-0 GP with qLogEI beats the log-plus-Sobol floor on the acceptance
tasks at matched measured budget, judged at the tier-0 gate's adjusted
level. Comparisons among GP variants, or across development tasks, are
secondary and corrected in the same family. Every entry's primary
endpoint, its family, and its adjusted level are written into
\texttt{validation/protocols.json} before its campaign begins, for the
same reason its thresholds are.

\subsection{Immutable artifacts and preregistration}

None of the above binds unless the plan provably predates the data,
so every gate campaign emits a signed manifest before it runs. The
manifest is JSON and carries the protocol version and entry
identifier (\texttt{V04-T0-v1.2} and the like), SHA-256 checksums of
task code, data files, and environment configuration, the exact
tuning and test seed sets per method and repetition, the analysis
script with its thresholds and tests committed to version control
while the results do not yet exist, a full dependency lock and
hardware description, the commit hash of the code under test, and a
creation timestamp with a signature.

\emph{Signed} and \emph{immutable} have to name mechanisms rather than
intentions, though, and an earlier draft of this section used both words
without either. A SHA-256 checksum establishes integrity, not
authenticity: it proves a file has not changed, not that the party who
declared it had the authority to. So the scheme is named—the manifest is
signed over a canonical JSON serialization, with a declared key owner, a
verification key published through a channel separate from the
repository, and a rotation procedure. Canonicalization matters more than
it sounds: the same manifest with reordered keys or different whitespace
hashes differently, so two honest parties can disagree about whether a
signature verifies. The dependency lock is machine-readable and reaches
past package versions to their hashes, the package indexes, the operating
system, the Python, the accelerator driver and toolkit, the container
image digest, and the cluster runtime.

Immutability has a matching gap. Git history is not immutable if anyone
can force-push, and a ten-gigabyte checkpoint is not governable by
ordinary Git at all. Large artifacts therefore live in a
content-addressed object store with a stated retention, backup, and
deletion policy, and the manifest carries the URI and digest rather than
the bytes. The order of operations is fixed too, since a manifest signed
after a run began proves nothing: commit the analysis, freeze the task
and seed registries, build and sign the manifest, verify it, and only
then start the campaign. A pre-run check refuses to launch when the
signature, the code commit, the task registry, or the dependency lock
fails to verify, which is what makes the ordering an enforced property
rather than a described one.

Results are written as versioned JSON with the manifest hash
embedded, under \texttt{validation/artifacts/}, a path deliberately
not ignored by \texttt{.gitignore}. Reanalysis is permitted and
expected; what it may not do is quietly replace the first analysis. A
changed threshold, a changed script, or a changed seed set after
results are visible produces a new manifest at an incremented version
with the reason written down, and both versions survive.

The point is not ceremony. Researcher degrees of freedom
\citep{simmons2011false} are invisible in a report and obvious in a
diff, and this is the cheapest available way to put them in a diff: a
reviewer can check that the analysis plan predates the data, that the
tested code matches the history, and that the tasks and seeds were
not chosen after the fact to flatter a result the team had already
grown attached to.

\section{The two expensive campaigns}
\label{sec:val-v08}

Two entries dominate the compute bill and deserve their design
stated in advance.

V07 races DEHB against BO+ASHA at a matched sixteen-full-run budget
on the pinned RL task, ten repetitions each. The cost driver is the
pinned task's full-run price, so the task is sized in week one with
ARLBench's guidance on representative subsets, which cut tuner
evaluation cost by roughly an order of magnitude in their study
\citep{becktepe2024arlbench}. As a sizing illustration, labeled an
estimate: if one full run costs two accelerator-hours on the chosen
environment, the campaign is $2 \times 16 \times 10 \times 2 = 640$
accelerator-hours, under a month on a single dedicated node and a
weekend on eight. If the chosen environment cannot fit that
envelope, the environment is wrong, not the protocol.

V08 is the flagship's trial, run late, when the population line
exists: the population method against DEHB and BO+ASHA on both sides
of the eight-worker boundary at matched total compute. It is the one
entry where the ten-repetition rule collides with the price of the
test, because a single population arm already costs eight
full-length trainings, and ten repetitions on both sides of the
boundary is a quarter-million accelerator-hour campaign we will not
buy. The exception is therefore stated as a protocol rather than
waved through. The repetition count drops to three per arm per side,
the smallest number that still yields a spread rather than a point,
and the reduction is paid for by narrowing the question: V08 does not
ask which method is best, only whether the boundary sits where
Chapter~\ref{sec:population} says it does. Three arms, two sides,
three repetitions, and the ordering of arms within a side is what the
entry reports, with the observed spread shown always.

The stopping rule is written in advance, because a cheap test with a
flexible endpoint is worse than no test. If all three repetitions on
a side agree in ordering, the side is called and no further
repetitions are bought. If they disagree, the entry reports
\emph{unresolved at this budget} and the flagship keeps whatever
status it had going in, which is a hold rather than a promotion; the
one outcome V08 may never produce is a promotion on evidence that did
not separate.

Agreement in ordering needs a definition, or the stopping rule inherits
the flexibility it was written to remove. The statistic is the mean
range-normalized difference against the worse of the two comparators,
computed per side. Two arms within two percent of each other on that
scale are a tie and count toward neither ordering. A side with fewer
than three completed repetitions is not callable at all and is re-run
rather than read. And consistent ordering alone does not demote: three
noisy repetitions pointing the same way is weak evidence of direction
and almost none of magnitude, so the shortfall must also exceed ten
percent, twice the decision margin, before the tripwire fires. The
probability that this rule fires when the flagship is in fact fine is
estimated by simulation from the pilot hierarchy and printed in the gate
report, because an asymmetric rule whose false-demotion rate nobody
publishes is an asymmetry nobody can weigh.

The regime the boundary divides is named as well: the home side is
sixteen workers, and the lower side is four. The lower side exists to
show that the population line is not winning merely by consuming
parallelism no sequential method was given, and a result there cannot
by itself call the entry either way. The demotion direction is asymmetric and deliberately
so: a consistent loss in the flagship's home regime demotes it on
three repetitions, because the claim being falsified is a strong one
and a strong claim can be broken by a weak test, whereas confirming
the boundary would need the full protocol we declined to buy. That
asymmetry is the honest content of a three-repetition campaign, and
it is why V08 is written here as a falsifier of
Chapter~\ref{sec:population}'s threshold rather than as its
validation.

The result states and their release consequences are named too, since
a falsifier with no defined pass state cannot gate a tier. V08 opens
at \emph{hold: insufficient evidence} and can reach three places.
\emph{Demote}: all three repetitions on a side agree that the
population line loses in its home regime, in which case the flagship
becomes an experimental option and the roadmap's defaults revert to
whichever of DEHB or BO+ASHA won V07. \emph{Hold}: the repetitions
disagree, or the home-regime side is inconclusive, in which case the
entry stays at \emph{unresolved at this budget} and the flagship
keeps its tier-2 experimental status without becoming a default
recommendation. \emph{Pass}: reachable only through the full
sequential campaign this entry declined to buy, at up to ten
repetitions per arm per side with preregistered margins and decision
boundaries. The three-repetition run is a tripwire, not promotion
evidence, and no accumulation of tripwires becomes one.

A fourth state has to be distinguished from the three above, because
conflating it with demotion would retire a method for a bug: the
population manager failing to run correctly is an
\emph{implementation failure}, not a method loss. It blocks the tier
until fixed and the campaign is re-run; it never demotes.

The demotion path also needs its fallback defined all the way down. If
the flagship demotes, defaults revert to whichever of DEHB or BO+ASHA
won V07—and if V07 tied or came back inconclusive, to DEHB. Naming that
tie-break here matters because V08's fallback rule points at V07's
outcome, and a rule that resolves to another entry's undefined state is
undefined itself at exactly the moment a gate needs it.

Tier~2 completion therefore requires that V08 be neither demoted nor
blocked by an implementation failure; a hold is acceptable
completion. That is the honest reading of a capability whose audience
is users at eight or more dedicated workers: the population line
ships as an option with Chapter~\ref{sec:population}'s boundary
documented as guidance rather than enforced as a default, and the
campaign that would promote it waits for tier~3 or a post-release
cycle, if demand and budget ever arrive together.

But \emph{waits for a post-release cycle, if demand and budget ever
arrive together} is how a hold becomes permanent by neglect, and a
capability parked next to a release decision with no owner and no
deadline is the failure mode this whole chapter is written against. A
hold therefore carries three things or it is not a hold: a named owner,
a dated expiry, and a funded follow-up campaign committed at the time
the hold is entered rather than sought afterwards. Until the powered
campaign runs, the population line ships opt-in only, with no default
dispatch on any workload. If the expiry passes without that campaign
completing, the entry auto-demotes to an option rather than lingering.
The hold does not block the internal beta gate, which is what makes the
expiry the load-bearing part: a state that costs nothing to hold and
never ends is not a decision, it is a deferral wearing a decision's
clothes.

\section{Two entries whose protocol needs pinning}

V10 and V13 both gate a capability on a threshold, so the threshold
has to be a number chosen before the data arrives rather than after.

V10 asks whether a short-horizon energy-drift measurement may cull a
Hamiltonian trial, and the question is a safety question rather than an
association question. An earlier draft answered the wrong one. It ran a
hundred configurations at a single fixed seed, measured drift at the
candidate rung and at the endpoint, and let MO-ASHA kill on the signal
whenever the bootstrap lower bound on the Spearman correlation cleared
$0.6$. Two things are wrong with that. A rank correlation, however high,
does not bound the probability that culling removes a configuration that
would have finished top-$k$ or Pareto-optimal: correlation describes the
middle of the distribution, and culling errors live in the tail where a
late-blooming configuration sits low at the rung. And one seed per
configuration confounds configuration quality with training-seed
variance, so the design cannot estimate a culling error rate even in
principle—there is no within-configuration variation left to estimate it
from.

The entry now measures the operational error directly. Configurations
are evaluated across five training seeds each, on several independent
tasks of the Hamiltonian family rather than one, and the gate reads two
bounds: the upper confidence bound on the probability of culling a
configuration that would have finished in the top ten must clear
$0.05$, and the lower bound on top-ten survivor recall must clear
$0.90$. Both must hold; clearing one is not a pass. The Spearman
correlation survives as a descriptive statistic, reported with its
interval and deciding nothing, and the normalized regret of the culled
set is a corrected secondary. The candidate rung is fixed before any
acceptance data is seen. Below the bounds the scheduler still runs but
the signal is advisory, promotion decisions falling back to the
objective that does correlate. The thresholds are judgments, not results
from the literature, and they are recorded here and in the protocol
register so that a later argument about them is an argument about a
written-down choice. The campaign is larger than the earlier
design—configurations times seeds times tasks—and is budgeted under
layer-two validation accordingly, which is the price of measuring the
error the scheduler can actually make.

V13 asks whether the sampler study's correctness vetoes hold. Three
components, each with its own rule. The vetoes themselves are
absolute: any configuration with a divergent transition or
split-$\widehat{R}$ above $1.01$ is never promoted, and a single
promotion of a veto-failing configuration fails the entry outright.
The sampling protocol that feeds the vetoes: four independent chains
per configuration, each from a dispersed random initialization, 2,000
warmup draws discarded plus 2,000 retained draws per chain, for 8,000
posterior samples total per configuration. Split-$\widehat{R}$ is
computed by splitting each chain in half (eight half-chains) and
comparing within-half-chain variance to between-half-chain variance
per the rank-normalized folded formula of \citet{vehtari2021rhat}; the
veto fires if any marginal exceeds $1.01$ or if effective sample size
falls below 400. Divergent transitions are reported by the sampler
itself (a numerical-integration failure during the Hamiltonian
trajectory). Bulk and tail effective sample size are both monitored, not
a single pooled figure, since a chain can carry ample bulk mass and
still resolve the tails too poorly to trust an interval.

Multimodality is detected by an automated diagnostic: mixture-component
detection on the pooled draws together with a between-chain mode
disagreement statistic, each against a threshold declared in the
protocol register. An earlier draft detected it by visual inspection of
marginal trace plots during the pilot and re-initialized the chains from
dispersed modes if anything looked bimodal. That is a researcher degree
of freedom sitting in the middle of a correctness test: the decision to
re-initialize depends on a judgment made after seeing the draws, and two
analysts inspecting the same traces need not reach the same conclusion.
The behavior it described is right—dispersed re-initialization is the
correct response to a multimodal target—so it survives as a
preregistered protocol branch that a declared statistic triggers, rather
than as a response to an inspection.

Survivors are ranked by effective sample size per gradient evaluation,
the cost measure the executor records rather than one the user
supplies. Correctness itself is established two ways. The first is
simulation-based calibration: over a declared set of fixtures with known
generating parameters, the rank of the true parameter within the
posterior draws must be uniform, checked against a simultaneous band on
the rank histogram. Calibration fixtures test the sampler against
ground truth rather than against another sampler, which is the only
check that can catch an error both implementations share.

The second is agreement with a reference posterior computed once at
great length using the same four-chain protocol but with 20,000
retained draws per chain. An earlier draft defined agreement as the
candidate's posterior mean falling within one standard error of the
reference mean. That criterion is too weak in three ways at once. It
treats the reference as exact when the reference is itself a finite
sample with its own Monte Carlo error; a one-standard-error band is a
roughly 68 percent interval, so a correct sampler fails it about a third
of the time; and applied to each marginal separately across many
marginals, it either flags a correct sampler somewhere by chance or, read
charitably, flags nothing at all. Agreement is now Monte Carlo standard
error aware and simultaneous: the band accounts for the sampling error of
both the candidate and the reference, and the criterion is applied
jointly across the monitored marginals rather than one at a time. The
reference artifact, the monitored marginal list, and the fixture set are
all frozen before any candidate is evaluated.

The vetoes are deliberately conservative, and conservatism has a price
worth naming: a hard veto will occasionally block a configuration that
was in fact fine. That false-veto rate is reported in the gate report
rather than left implicit, because a safety threshold whose error rate
nobody publishes is a threshold nobody can argue with. An entry that
ranks a fast, subtly wrong sampler above a slow, correct one is the
failure this test exists to catch, which is why speed enters only after
the vetoes, the calibration check, and the agreement check have passed.

\section{Sabotage entries}
\label{sec:sabotage}

Three entries test the testers. V03 seeds defects into the wiring, an
off-by-one rung, a dropped seed, a flipped objective sign, and
requires the parity and replay layers to catch them; a suite that
cannot catch a planted bug is decoration, and this entry is the
reason we can trust green elsewhere. V11 seeds bad advice, the
purposefully detrimental prior in $\pi$BO's own sense, and requires
the mechanism's safety half, recovery to the prior-free baseline, to
hold on our tasks and budgets, not only in the paper
(Chapter~\ref{sec:transfer}). V16 audits the validators themselves:
it requires that every validator fail when given structurally empty
input (zero trials, zero events, zero mutants), that performance
thresholds be recorded before data rather than tuned afterward, and
that parity validators compare against their declared reference rather
than against self. The tier-0 gate initially recorded a pass on
validators that failed all three criteria; V16 closes that class of
drift.

\section{What the suite costs, and when it runs}

Layer one is effectively free and runs on every change: minutes of
CPU per full pass, which is why it can gate merges. Layer three is
free by construction. Layer two is a budget line: V04, V05, V06,
V09, V10, V11, and V13 together are dominated by the RL entries and
the ten-repetition rule; on the sizing illustration above, a full
tier-1 gate pass is of the order of a few hundred accelerator-hours,
scheduled as a campaign with a manifest, not run ad hoc. V07 and V08
are separate, priced campaigns. All layer-two numbers are estimates
until the week-one task sizing; the envelope, one GPU-week class of
compute per tier gate, is the planning figure the calendar of
Chapter~\ref{sec:roadmap} carries.

\section{Gates, and what failure does}

A tier is done when its register entries are green, and each entry's
failure has a named consequence rather than a shrug. Wiring failures
(V01--V03, V14) block the tier and are repaired. Evidence failures
execute the demotion already promised in the verdict's chapter:
V04 removes the losing searcher from defaults, V09 demotes the
premium searcher to an option, V11 demotes priors to opt-in, V08
demotes the flagship. The register of
\texttt{product\_register.json} records, for every verdict, which
entry anchors it; a verdict whose entry fails does not quietly
survive in the code while dying in the appendix.

\section{What would overturn this chapter}

The suite is itself under test. If V03's planted defects pass, the
parity layer is rebuilt before anything else proceeds. If the
layer-two envelope exceeds its planning figure by more than a small
factor after week-one sizing, the pinned tasks are resized and this
chapter's estimates corrected in place. And if layer three's
standing diagnostics show our real studies violating the
assumptions the pinned tasks embody, fidelities decorrelating where
the suite assumed they correlate, the pinned tasks follow the
evidence, because a suite that drifts from the workloads it guards
inherits exactly the proxy bias it was built to catch.
